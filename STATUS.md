## The alignment tolerance gated on noise — R40, 2026-09-01

R37 §3.3 set the tolerance at one tick of the parent clock. Measured, that is 32.0 µs, and the
estimator it gates costs **31 µs at the median one anchor interval out** — so the default sat
*at* the typical error, and this session passed it at 0.96 ticks against 1.00. A 4% margin, on
measurement noise, deciding whether the first 1.73 anchor intervals of every stream are kept or
stranded.

Extending R37 §2's measurement past its sixty seconds, on twelve probe clocks:

| intervals out | ≈ s | p50 | p95 | max |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1 | 31.4 µs | 66.9 µs | 837 µs |
| **2** — what this session needs | 2 | 30.8 µs | **116.5 µs** | 1611 µs |
| 10 | 10 | 58.8 µs | 112.2 µs | 1626 µs |
| 60 | 60 | 53.8 µs | 386.3 µs | 1688 µs |
| 120 | 120 | 60.7 µs | 804.6 µs | 1711 µs |

Two minutes costs twice one second. The error is flat in distance, which is R37 §2's finding
confirmed an order of magnitude further out, and it means the tolerance should be set against
the estimator's floor rather than against how far it is asked to reach.

**R40** makes it 3 ms, stated as a duration and judged at **p95** rather than the median. That
is 26× the cost of what this session actually needs, and still 10× tighter than the finest PSTH
bin in the analysis capsules this format was measured against. A clock with three anchors, which
is what the gate exists for, misses by seconds and still fails.

**And a dropped row is now counted, always.** Nothing counted them before, which is why a reader
mapping at bound 1 and a writer materializing at bound 2 could disagree about which spikes exist
— two of one unit's 40,774 — without either saying so. The count goes in the producing run's
parameters even when it is zero, because a derived table one row short looks exactly like a
derived table, and a recorded zero is the evidence the bound was sufficient.

## Checked against the NWB packaging, 2026-09-01

`aind-ephys-export-nwb` has to solve the same synchronization and solves it the other way, which
makes it the independent check this format did not otherwise have. It loads
`continuous/<stream>/timestamps.npy` — one float64 per sample, rewritten to HARP by
`aind-ephys-rig-qc` — then `recording.set_times(sync_times)` and
`sorting.register_recording(recording)`, so a spike time is a lookup at the spike's frame.

|  | AIND | here |
|---|---|---|
| representation | 191,634,463 float64 per stream | 6,387 anchors per stream |
| size, twelve streams | 18.4 GB | 76,646 rows |
| spike time | array lookup at the frame | piecewise-linear between anchors (R13) |
| outside the anchored region | a number, always | a mask saying undefined (R27 §5.3, R37 §3.3) |
| a bad upstream anchor | baked into every sample | withheld and reported (R37 §3.2) |

**They agree to 9 µs median and 24 µs p95 over 263,094 spikes** — a quarter of a probe sample,
and consistent with our rounding to integer HARP ticks being the whole of the residual.

**And where they disagree, the disagreement is diagnostic.** Compare the HARP time of sample 0:
the two streams whose first anchor R37 §3.2 kept agree to **0.1 and 0.8 µs**, so six thousand

first barcode, so the dense array computes it with the global fit and the defect becomes data.

had measured and materialized at twice that, so reading `core` dropped the earliest spikes that

the packet records.

## An inherited window rule is a join, not a CASE — measured 2026-09-01

R38 §2.5 says checking a unit against its clock's inherited window is cheap. The *semantics*
are: 13 windows rather than 6,781. The first SQL written for it was not, and the gap between
those two statements is worth recording, because R12 §4 already says a rule can be written the
wrong way and this is the other failure mode it warns about.

The rule as first written asked, per spike, `CASE WHEN EXISTS (own window) THEN NOT EXISTS
(own window containing t_tick) WHEN EXISTS (clock window) THEN NOT EXISTS (clock window
containing t_tick) ELSE FALSE END`. Against 286,896,563 spikes under a 20 GB limit:

```text
CASE, four correlated subqueries   > 9 min, 65 GB spilled, killed before finishing
two top-level EXISTS               > 4 min, 22 GB spilled, killed before finishing
explicit JOIN + LEFT JOIN            1.4 s,  no spill
```

`t_tick` appears inside each subquery, so it is a *correlated* column, and flattening a
dependent join materializes the distinct correlated tuples — which here is the spike table.
Hoisting the subqueries to top level does not help: the correlation is still there. Written as
joins there is no correlation at all, and both sides are what they should be — a 6,781-row
build and a streaming 287 M-row probe.

The negative control matters as much as the timing: shifting every window one second later
makes the same query report offending spikes in 1.3 s, so the rewrite is fast because it is a
better plan and not because it stopped asking. Zero spikes are outside their window on this
session, which is R38 §2.5's claim over the whole table rather than at the ends.

**The rule of thumb this leaves:** a Tier 2 rule over a table of hundreds of millions of rows
must not correlate a subquery on a per-row column. Join to the small side instead.

## Spike-table physical layout, measured 2026-08-28

Against the real 248 M-row table, closing R5 §8's open question — `conformance/parquet-probe/`
had only synthetic uniform data, and `real.py` there now reproduces this.

**The two tables have different byte profiles and conclusions do not transfer between them.**
`core.spike_events` carries `amplitude`, incompressible under any order and 23% of its bytes;
`analysis.spike_events` does not, and is 98.8% `t_tick`. An earlier entry here concluded from
core that the two sort orders cost the same (784 vs 789 MB) and that delta was 10-30% slower on
selective reads. Neither survives on the analysis table — amplitude was masking the difference.

```text
core      783 MB   t_tick 592.7 (76%)   amplitude 182.6 (23%)   5 strings 8.1 (1%)
analysis  594 MB   t_tick 586.4 (98.8%)                         5 strings 7.1 (1.2%)
```

**Superseded 2026-08-29: `core.spike_events` now uses `DELTA_BINARY_PACKED` too.** The
"delta is 10-30% slower on selective reads" finding above does not hold against the encoding
pyarrow actually picks by default. A tick column has too many distinct values for a dictionary,
so it overflows the dictionary page and falls back to `PLAIN` mid-column — paying the
bookkeeping and then storing eight bytes anyway, which is why the old files list
`(PLAIN, RLE, RLE_DICTIONARY)` for one column chunk. Measured on the full 287 M-row table:

```text
              t_tick            spike table   packet   staging   validation
dictionary    665.5 MB  2.32 B/row   885.0 MB   871 MB    54.7 s     32.4 s
delta         462.0 MB  1.61 B/row   681.6 MB   677 MB    50.1 s     31.9 s
```

Smaller, faster to write and faster to read at once; a filtered read is unchanged. Sorting by
unit makes the tick reset once per unit — 801 descents in 20 M rows — and delta absorbs that,
because the run between resets is tens of thousands of ascending values.

### Encoding

Dictionary on `t_tick` is the worst option measured, at every row-group size and in both
orders, and is what the writer emits today: it builds a dictionary nearly as large as the
column, overflows the 1 MB page limit, abandons it mid-column, and emits both.

```text
analysis.spike_events, 247,758,402 rows
U-dict-1M    593.9 MB  19.18 bit/row      T-dict-1M   895.9 MB  28.93 bit/row
U-plain-1M   531.6      17.16             T-plain-1M  789.4      25.49
U-delta-1M   426.4      13.77             T-delta-1M  688.0      22.21
```

Compression barely matters once delta is applied, so zstd level is irrelevant.

### Entropy is conserved; sorting moves it between columns

```text
                 t_tick  unit_id  sorting_run  curation    total
U-delta-1M        13.53     0.06      0.05        0.06     13.76 bit/row
H300-delta-128K   ~14.3     ~0.2      ~0.05       ~0.06    14.63
T-delta-1M         1.87    13.14      3.56        3.56     22.21
```

Time-major drives `t_tick` to 1.87 bit/row — consecutive spikes across 7,291 units are ~0.8
ticks apart — and pays 20.3 bits for the context unit-major run-length-encodes into 0.17. The
time-sorted copy is 61% larger, not the same size.

### What prunes, and what does not

- **Sort order.** ~100x under PyArrow, the read path a packet is required to have. DuckDB
  shows 1.5-36x only because it bottoms out on a ~0.2 s fixed cost at this scale.
- **Row groups above 1 M rows.** Nothing; 1 M and 8 M are byte- and time-identical.
- **Bloom filters on `unit_id`.** Useless, and measurably so — a time-major row group holds
  6,651-6,874 of ~7,291 units, so a filter can never exclude one. 0.5% overhead for nothing.
- **Page index.** PyArrow does not read it. DuckDB gained on a full scan, from the smaller
  pages rather than from the index.
- **Small row groups.** Nearly free *once `t_tick` is not dictionary-encoded* — 128 K costs
  0.6% with delta against 73% with dictionary. That is what makes finer pruning purchasable.

### One table can serve both predicates

Bucketing time coarsely and sorting by unit within each bucket leaves both columns with
informative row-group statistics. The bucket is a stored `time_bucket` column, not an
expression, because `03` declares order as a column list and R12's shim verifies against it.

PyArrow, warm seconds, 247.8 M rows:

```text
                    MB   24 units  10 s win  24 scattered  units+win
U-delta-1M         426      0.026     0.764        0.234       0.032
U-delta-128K       429      0.030     0.301        0.056       0.041
T-delta-1M         688      2.606     0.008        2.586       0.030
H60-delta-128K     511      0.099     0.049        1.039       0.044
H300-delta-128K    453      0.045     0.045        0.319       0.047
H300-delta-32K     462      0.107     0.155        0.195       0.141
```

For a hybrid, a time window must read the whole bucket regardless of row-group size, while a
unit query reads about one row group per bucket. So time cost scales with bucket size and unit
cost with (bucket count x row-group size); their product is fixed by row-group size alone, and
shrinking row groups improves the unit query for free until metadata overhead takes over.

The conjunction — selected units within a window — is 30-47 ms in every layout, so it does not
motivate a second copy. Neither `U-delta-128K` nor `H300-delta-128K` dominates: the hybrid is
6.7x faster on a time window and 5.7x slower on scattered units, at 6% more bytes.

### `core` cannot adopt a bucketed layout

Not a trade-off. `core.spike_events` has no common time axis to bucket on — its `t_tick` is on
each probe's own clock, twelve here, and `t_tick // N` is meaningful only within one clock. R27
lets a counter that resets mid-session become two clocks; R13 and R27 make the conversion
piecewise and anchor-dependent. A reference-clock bucket column would put a derived,
anchor-dependent value into the layer that holds measured facts and make it load-bearing for
the file's sort order, going stale whenever anchors are revised. Per-clock buckets are
well-defined but require the anchors to be applied before the buckets are usable, which is
what the analysis layer exists to have done once.

The time-clustered layout is not something core declines; it is something core cannot express.
That is the same boundary R30 draws.

### Decided

- `core.spike_events` keeps `03`'s unit-major order and 1 M row groups. Only the encoding
  changes: dictionary -> `DELTA_BINARY_PACKED` (783 -> 610 MB, −22%), or -> `PLAIN` (~10%) if
  R3 §2.1's native-reader argument is to be preserved. Parked pending the Julia question.
- `analysis.spike_events` takes delta unconditionally.
- `03` mandates nothing about encodings and calls row-group size a starting point to "adjust
  after profiling", so none of this needs a revision. Changing the *order* would.

### Decided and built, 2026-08-28

`analysis.spike_events` is **one** bucketed resource, not two sorted copies: 300 s buckets,
`DELTA_BINARY_PACKED` on `t_tick`, 128 K-row row groups. Written up as R30 (proposed).

### The packet is at version 0.2.0

`PacketWriter.amend()` implements R32 and the table is **in the packet**, not beside it:

```text
amend: passed, 0 error(s), 0 finding(s) in 4.7 min, peak 11,322 MiB
0.1.0 -> 0.2.0, supersedes 0.1.0, registry 0.1.0 -> 0.2.0, 10 -> 11 resources
(the reference registry is back at 0.1.0; this packet embeds the 0.2.0 it was built against)

schemas/    logical_schemas.0.1.0.json  logical_schemas.json
sessions, description and created_at all carried over from the superseded manifest
```

1.2 GB on disk. The analysis table's primary key is now a generated Tier 1 check like any
other, rather than something checked by hand.

Three defects the implementation surfaced, all fixed:

- **`schema_id` was derived from `registry_version`.** A schema ID names the *logical
  standard* — `core.trials.v0.1` — not the registry, so a registry revision would have
  renamed every schema in the packet. Fixed to read `logical_standard_version`. Found by
  bumping the registry to 0.2.0 for the amendment; the version has since been **held back at
  0.1.0** while the schema is still moving, since version discipline is Phase 6 and bumping on
  every revision numbers churn rather than releases.

  That leaves one live hazard, deliberately: two registries at 0.1.0 with different content
  cannot be told apart, which is exactly what R2 §8 relies on. `_install_registry` refuses
  that collision rather than letting it into a packet, so it surfaces at the boundary where it
  matters. The committed packet embeds `0.2.0` and keeps working regardless — a packet
  carrying its own registry is precisely what R2 §8 is for, including when the reference moves
  backwards during pre-release iteration.
- **The registry has to be installed before validation, not after.** A validator checks the
  embedded copy against the version the manifest declares (R2 §9), so an amendment declaring
  the new version against the old copy fails on a mismatch that says nothing about the data.
  `amend` now installs it first and restores it on refusal — verified on the real packet,
  which came back at 0.1.0 with no stray retained copy.
- **`commit` and `amend` had no way to bound validation.** The validator's 4 GB default is not
  enough once a packet holds two 10^8-row tables: `core.spike_events.primary_key_unique`
  reported *"check could not run: Out of Memory"*, which is not a statement about the data.
  Both now take `limits`; this ran at 9 GB.

Two revisions were forced by the work and are now **accepted**: **R31** maps `06`'s
materialization activity onto `core.processing_runs` plus the manifest's `processing_run_id`,
and **R32** defines the amendment itself.

**R31 is implemented.** `materialize.into` emits the run, `PacketWriter.produced_by` records
the link, and `_check_materialization_provenance` is the shim check R31 §5 specifies — not
SQL, because `processing_run_id` lives in the manifest and no query sees it. A Tier 2 rule
covers the row's own fields, which *are* SQL's business: a `materialization` run without
`software_name` and `software_version` is an error. The packet is at **0.3.0**:

```text
processing_run_id: materialize.analysis.spike_events
run: materialization  escapewheel_writer 0.1.0
     {"bound":1.0,"bucket_seconds":300,"reference_clock_id":null}
```

`amend` also had to learn that "add rows to a provenance table" (R32 §3) means *add*: a
resource is staged from whatever the writer holds, so amending with one new run would have
rewritten the table as that single row and dropped every earlier one. It now reads the
existing rows back in and refuses a primary-key collision, which would be a revision rather
than an addition.

### The packet carries two registries called 0.1.0

Holding `registry_version` at 0.1.0 across a schema change did exactly the damage predicted
when the decision was taken. The packet now holds:

```text
schemas/logical_schemas.0.1.0.json   0.1.0   274 cols   47 rules   (retained at amendment 1)
schemas/logical_schemas.0.2.0.json   0.2.0   275 cols   49 rules   (retained at amendment 2)
schemas/logical_schemas.json         0.1.0   275 cols   50 rules   (current)
```

Reading the packet at 0.3.0 is unaffected — the canonical path and the manifest agree. What
broke is the *history*: retained manifest 0.1.0 names registry 0.1.0, and two different things
now answer to that name, so the state it describes cannot be reconstructed. That is precisely
what R32 §4's retention exists to prevent.

The version is **held at 0.1.0** while the schema is unreleased, which is safe for a reason
rather than by luck: the ambiguity R2 §8 guards against needs a packet to retain *two*
registries, and only an amendment does that. A packet built fresh embeds exactly one, so no
version can be ambiguous inside it. `_install_registry` refuses the amendment that would
create the ambiguity — checking retained copies, not merely the current one, which is the
narrowness that let this packet through — so the bump happens when it is forced rather than
on a schedule.

`packet_version` moves at patch level too (`0.1.0 -> 0.1.1 -> ...`). R32 §2 requires only that
it sort strictly after the superseded version and §8 leaves its meaning open, so nothing rests
on the choice. It cannot be frozen outright: `supersedes` chains on it and retained manifests
are named by it, so a fixed value would overwrite the history retention exists to keep.

This packet stays at 0.3.0 with the collision until it is rebuilt. A rebuild starts a fresh
`packet_version` at 0.1.0 and writes one registry, which resolves both.

**The population time slice is the structural result.** An NWB stores spikes as one ragged
array per unit, so there is no ordering over time to prune on and a window is a post-filter
over all 287 M spikes; R30's bucketed layout reads one bucket. 478x.

lazynwb wins `open + header` because it has no open step -- it is lazy by construction, while
a packet parses a manifest.

The one count that differs, 349,551 against 349,550, is not an error on either side: the two
alignments disagree by 65-154 us (below), so a spike at the window's edge falls inside for one
and outside for the other.

### Materialised or converted on the fly

`analysis.spike_events` is bucketed by time, so a unit's spikes scatter across every bucket;
`core.spike_events` is unit-major and contiguous. Reading core and aligning at read time:

```text
                    materialised   on the fly
1 unit's train            0.055s       0.034s   on the fly 1.6x faster
24 units' trains          0.075s       0.096s   materialised 1.3x faster
10 s, all units           0.043s          n/a   core has no common clock to window on
```

So the arithmetic is not what the materialisation buys -- aligning 137 K spikes costs about
5 ms, and 2.4 M about 20 ms. It buys the **layout**, and only for time-window access, which is
exactly what R30 §1 claims and now has its negative half measured too. It also means a reader
that synchronises by default would be competitive with a materialised copy, so the safe
default need not be the slow one.

### Three things the numbers do not say

- **The packet is 1.39 GB against the NWB's 8.49 GB, and that is not a fair comparison.** The
  NWB carries `waveform_mean` and `waveform_sd`, rank 3 and several GB, which the packet does
  not carry at all -- dense arrays are Phase 5. The timing rows name their columns so both
  sides fetch the same thing; the size figure has no such control.
- **Local disk, not Code Ocean.** `/data` FUSE latency dominates there, and the capsule's own
  notes warn against measuring locally for that reason. Local is right for comparing *formats*;
  these ratios will not transfer to a capsule.
- **lazynwb's idiom re-reads the units table per call** -- `get_df`, filter, then
  `merge_array_column`, which its documentation recommends. That read is 0.022 s of the
  0.909 s, so hoisting it out of a loop would not rescue much; the cost is the merge.

### The clock chain, checked against the pipeline's own arithmetic

The packet maps each probe's sample counter onto HARP through `core.clock_anchors` (R13, R27).
The NWB carries the same spikes already aligned, by a pipeline that did its own arithmetic.
Neither was derived from the other:

```text
unit                          ours     theirs    offset       spread
...46110.u000              137,260    137,260   +0.000000 s   154.1 us
...46116-3.u245              2,516      2,516   +0.000000 s    65.2 us
...45883-3.u047              6,879      6,879   +0.000000 s    80.4 us

analysis t_tick 361,746,131,857..361,945,753,098 = 11575876.219..11582264.099 s
nwb spike_times                                    11575876.219..11582264.099 s
```

Identical counts, zero systematic offset, and the tick range converts to the second range
digit for digit. Put the 65-154 us spread beside the other alignment number measured today:
the rig's two record nodes, aligning the *same* NI-DAQ events independently, disagree by 70 us.
Our disagreement with the pipeline is the same order as the pipeline's with itself.

### What the benchmark found about our own format

Decoding a per-spike string column is the recurring cost, hit three separate ways:

- Materialising `core.spike_events.unit_id` for 287 M spikes decodes ~10 GB of strings --
  about a megabyte on disk, where it dictionary-encodes away. It OOM-killed a 24 GB cap in
  six seconds.
- Reading the per-spike `clock_id` to group 2.4 M spikes by clock cost 0.595 s against 0.096 s
  for looking the clock up once per unit from `core.units`. A clock is a property of the unit;
  the per-spike column restates it 287 M times.
- The withdrawn `GROUP BY` uniqueness check spilled 26 GB for the same reason.

Predicate-pushed reads never pay it, which is why 24 units cost 75 ms. Whole-column reads and
aggregates do.

## Working alongside Aind.Behavior.VrForaging.Packaging

Most AIND behaviour data will arrive through that pipeline, so the two packages should meet
rather than converge by accident. `inventory/vr-foraging-packaging.md` is the Stage 1 read of
it; what follows is what came out of that.

**Landed 2026-08-31.** A shared `SourceStore` per root, so three adapters reading one `.nwb`
cannot disagree about it; declared compensations in `core.processing_runs.parameters`, so a
packet says what the importer assumed about its sources; and `--strict`, which refuses a build
on any adapter note.

**Next, in order.**

- **A `vrf` source adapter, reading their parquet rather than their NWB.** The NWB is a lossy
  projection of the same processors, and there is no VR-foraging NWB on this machine to read
  anyway. Cached exports exist at `single-site-curriculum-evaluation/scratch/dashboard_artifacts`
  — 213 sessions over ten mice, five streams — but that is an LRU cache keyed by *absolute
  session path*, already at its 2 GB per-stream cap and evicting, with no `events` stream and
  some entries built by a mismatched parser. Copy what is needed out of it; do not read it in
  place. This is the item that turns everything below from opinion into measured friction.
- **An R17 extension registry generated from their pydantic `Site` model.** The real alignment:
  generated in their repo, their schema change updates our validation, and they get Tier 1
  checks for tables now guarded by pytest alone. Needs a conversation, not code.
- **Ask them to declare the clock.** Their tables are harp seconds and nothing *in the data*
  says 31,250 Hz or where `t = 0` is; it is prose in a docstring. We cannot build `core.clocks`
  from prose. Nearly free now, awkward once published. Measured while asking: their float64
  seconds are *not* lossy — the ULP is 1.5% of a tick even a century out — so the ask is to
  state the rate, not to change the representation.
- **Identity in the parquet footer.** `packet_id`, `packet_version`, `resource_id`,
  `registry_version` as key-value metadata, checked against the manifest so a divergence is a
  finding. Weaker for us than for them: they have no manifest and we do. Two measured caveats —
  `store_schema=False` drops footer metadata as well, so `analysis.spike_events` cannot carry
  it; and their own cached exports carry *no* provenance, because the Dashboard's write path
  bypasses the promotion `process_session` does. File metadata is per-writer discipline where a
  manifest is structural, which is the argument against making it load-bearing.

**Deferred, not dropped.**

- **Version *dispatch*** — swapping adapter variants on a source's schema version, as their
  `_LEGACY_VERSION_CUTOFF` does. The compensation record above is the half that pays now; a
  dispatch table waits until a second vintage of some source actually exists, because until
  then it is a mechanism with one branch.
- **`nwbize()`-style co-located NWB projection** — one object owning both the table and its NWB
  representation, so they cannot drift. Only matters when `00`'s NWB projection is built, which
  is itself deferred. Noted as the pattern to use then.

**Considered and skipped.**

- **`--only` / `--skip` by resource.** Their `filter_processors` works because their tables are
  loosely coupled. Forty-six foreign keys mean a partial build here produces something
  deliberately invalid, and `--sample` already gives the fast development cycle without that.
- **Processor-per-table restructuring.** Its three benefits are independent testability (our
  `records()` dict has it), parallel fan-out (does not pay — `core.spike_events` is 99.9% of a
  session's rows), and selection by name, which is the skipped item above.
- **Per-table provenance** duplicating `core.processing_runs`, which fails R3 §3.0's test. The
  footer *identity* above is narrower and survives it.

**Held.** Integer ticks. Their float seconds are recoverable here, but by luck of magnitude
rather than construction: float seconds hid a 268-day LFP error and a 70 ms per-probe drift
that integer ticks made impossible to express.
